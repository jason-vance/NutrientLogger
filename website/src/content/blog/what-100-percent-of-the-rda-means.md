I've spent a lot of posts picking at the nutrient bar. That it has [a ceiling as well as a target](/blog/nutrient-upper-limits). That it shows [what you ate, not what you absorbed](/blog/bioavailability-what-you-actually-absorb). That the number filling it is [sometimes an estimate](/blog/where-the-numbers-come-from).

I never asked the most basic question about it. What is the 100% mark?

Most people read it as a pass line. Under 100% you're short, over 100% you're fine. That reading is wrong in a specific way, and the correct one is more useful.

## Where the number comes from

The default targets in my app, like those in any tracker built on the standard US reference tables, are Recommended Dietary Allowances. RDAs are set by expert committees at the National Academies, and the RDA is not the number a committee starts with.

It starts with the Estimated Average Requirement, or EAR. That's the daily intake estimated to meet the needs of half the healthy people in an age and sex group. At that intake, the other half aren't getting enough.

So the committee adds a margin. The RDA is the EAR plus two standard deviations, which is enough to cover 97 to 98% of healthy people. When there isn't enough data to know how much needs vary from person to person, the committee assumes a 10% coefficient of variation, and the RDA works out to 1.2 times the EAR.

**The RDA is what someone near the top of the range needs, assigned to everyone.** That's a sensible way to set a goal, because nobody knows where in the range they sit. It also means most people need less than their RDA, by definition.

## How big the margin is

Here are both numbers for a man aged 31 to 50:

| Nutrient | EAR | RDA | EAR as % of RDA |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Magnesium | 350 mg | 420 mg | 83% |
| Vitamin C | 75 mg | 90 mg | 83% |
| Zinc | 9.4 mg | 11 mg | 85% |
| Vitamin B12 | 2.0 µg | 2.4 µg | 83% |
| Folate | 320 µg DFE | 400 µg DFE | 80% |
| Vitamin A | 625 µg RAE | 900 µg RAE | 69% |

For these six, the average requirement sits between 69% and 85% of the 100% mark.

Iron for women is the extreme case. For women aged 19 to 50 the EAR is 8.1 mg and the RDA is 18 mg. Menstrual iron losses vary a lot from one woman to the next, so the requirement isn't a tidy bell curve. The committee modeled the whole distribution and set the RDA at its 97.5th percentile. The result is an RDA more than double the EAR.

## How to read a bar that's under 100%

These reference values are shared by the US and Canada, and Health Canada publishes a short summary of how to use them to assess one person's diet. Its three rules:

- Usual intake **at or above the RDA** has a low probability of being inadequate.
- Usual intake **below the EAR** very likely needs to improve. The odds that it's enough for you are 50% or worse.
- Usual intake **between the two** probably needs to improve.

So 90% is not a failing grade. It's more likely enough than not. It also isn't a guarantee, which is why the guidance still says to nudge it up. A bar that sits at 50% is a different situation. That's below the average requirement for every nutrient in the table above.

The word doing the work is "usual." The same summary warns that an average over a few days may not be accurate enough to judge by. It's one more reason to read the week and not the day.

And low intake is not the same thing as deficiency. The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements says many Americans get less magnesium than the recommended amounts. The same fact sheet says symptomatic magnesium deficiency is uncommon in healthy people. Both are true at once. A food log measures what's on the plate. It can't diagnose anything, and if a number worries you, that's a question for a doctor or a dietitian.

## Some of the bars aren't RDAs at all

When there isn't enough evidence to set an EAR, there's no RDA to calculate. The committee publishes an Adequate Intake instead. For vitamin K, potassium and manganese, the AI is based on how much healthy people were observed to eat.

That changes what a low bar means. At or above an AI, the reading is the same as for an RDA: low probability of a problem. Below an AI, the guidance is blunt. Adequacy "cannot be assessed." There's no EAR underneath to say how far short is too far.

Look back at [my two carnivore days](/blog/a-carnivore-day-in-micronutrients). Four of the seventeen rows in that table are AIs: vitamin K, choline, potassium and manganese. Day 1 showed magnesium at 35% and manganese at 4%. Those two bars look alike, and they're different kinds of evidence.

Magnesium at 35% is far below the average requirement. Manganese at 4% is far below what healthy people usually eat, measured against a requirement nobody has pinned down. The NIH fact sheet says manganese deficiency is very rare in humans and that its signs haven't been firmly established. That's what I meant in [the first carnivore post](/blog/carnivore-diet-micronutrient-gaps) when I said manganese requirements aren't well established. I still think a 4% bar is worth knowing about. I hold it more loosely than the magnesium one.

AIs also move. In 2019 the National Academies set the potassium AI at 3,400 mg for men and 2,600 mg for women, lower than the values from 2005.

## The label uses a different 100%

The % Daily Value on a Nutrition Facts panel is not a percentage of your RDA. The FDA sets one Daily Value per nutrient for everyone aged 4 and up, and for vitamins and minerals it generally takes the highest RDA (or AI) of any group in that range.

For most people that puts the label's 100% at or above their own RDA, sometimes well above:

- **Iron.** The DV is 18 mg, which is the RDA for women aged 19 to 50. A man's RDA is 8 mg, so a food showing 45% DV covers his full RDA.
- **Calcium.** The DV is 1,300 mg, which is the RDA for ages 9 to 18. For adults aged 19 to 50 it's 1,000 mg.
- **Potassium.** The DV is still 4,700 mg, well above the current AI for men or women.

The FDA's own rule of thumb treats %DV as a comparison tool: 5% or less per serving is low, 20% or more is high. It's built for choosing between two boxes on a shelf, not for grading your day.

Nutrient Logger's default targets are the RDAs (or AIs) for your age and sex, and Premium lets you set your own target for any nutrient. So the percentage in the app and the percentage printed on the package can disagree about the same food. Neither is wrong. They're dividing by different numbers.

## What I'd do with this

**Don't chase 100% on every bar every day.** It's a generous target by design. A weekly average in the 90s is a small nudge, not an alarm.

**Take a bar that stays low seriously.** Weeks at half your RDA puts you under the average requirement for most nutrients. That's worth changing what you eat.

**Know which kind of target you're looking at.** A low bar against an AI is a softer signal than a low bar against an RDA.

The 100% mark looks like a line between enough and not enough. It's really a point near the top of the range of what people need, and no tracker can see where in that range you sit. What a tracker can show you is how your intake compares with that range, week after week. That's a smaller claim than a full bar seems to make, and it's the one the numbers support.
