Every nutrition app on the market does the same thing. You log your food, and it tells you: calories, protein, carbs, fat. Maybe fiber if you're lucky.

That's it. That's the whole product.

And look - those numbers aren't useless. If your goal is to lose weight or build muscle, calories and macros matter. I'm not arguing otherwise.

But if your goal is to actually be *healthy*? Calories are the least interesting number on the label.

## The gap hiding in plain sight

You can eat 2,000 perfectly macro-balanced calories every day and still be deficient in magnesium, zinc, potassium, vitamin D, vitamin K2, or any number of nutrients your body needs to function. Macros tell you about *energy*. They tell you almost nothing about *nourishment*.

This isn't a fringe concern. Studies consistently show that large portions of the population are deficient in key micronutrients - not because they're not eating enough food, but because they're not eating the *right* food. Or because they have no idea what's actually in the food they're eating beyond the calorie count.

Think about it this way: a 400-calorie meal of grilled beef liver with butter is a completely different nutritional event than a 400-calorie protein bar. The calories are identical. The macros might even be close. But one is arguably the most nutrient-dense food on the planet, and the other is a processed product that might check a protein box while leaving you short on a dozen other things your body is quietly asking for.

Calorie-counting apps treat those two meals as roughly equivalent. They're not.

## Why the industry is stuck on calories

The honest answer is that calories are easy. Easy to count, easy to display, easy to build an app around. You need one number from a food label and you're done.

Micronutrients are harder. There are dozens of them. The data is messier. The USDA database has deep nutrient profiles for whole foods, but packaged food labels often only list a handful. Building a tracker that handles 80+ nutrients across thousands of foods is a different kind of engineering problem than summing up calories.

So most apps don't bother. They give you the calorie counter and call it a nutrition app.

I think that's selling people short.

## What I actually care about when I eat

I'll be transparent about where I'm coming from: I eat an animal-based diet. Quality meats, organs, dairy, fruit, honey. I'm influenced by Paul Saladino's work and I'm skeptical of a lot of conventional nutrition advice - not out of contrarianism, but because I've looked at the data and found the mainstream recommendations wanting in some areas.

That's my bias. Take the rest of this with that context.

When I log my food, calories are background noise. What I actually want to know is:

- **Am I getting enough magnesium?** Most people aren't, and it affects everything from sleep to muscle function.
- **Where's my zinc-to-copper ratio?** This matters more than most people realize.
- **Am I hitting my B vitamins?** Especially B12, folate, and B6 - the ones that quietly affect your energy and mood.
- **What about fat-soluble vitamins?** A, D, E, K - the ones you need adequate fat intake to even absorb properly.
- **Am I getting enough potassium relative to sodium?** The ratio matters more than the absolute sodium number everyone obsesses over.

None of this shows up in a standard calorie-counting app. You'd never know you were deficient until symptoms appeared, and by then, you've been running on empty for months.

## The food pyramid's long shadow

Part of the problem is that we've inherited decades of nutrition advice that focused on the wrong things. The food pyramid told us to eat 6-11 servings of grains per day and minimize fat. It treated all calories within a food group as roughly equal. It said nothing meaningful about micronutrient density.

We've moved past the food pyramid officially, but its influence lingers in every app that treats nutrition as a calorie math problem. The entire framework of "eat less, move more, count your calories" is a simplification that made sense as public health messaging but falls apart as personalized nutrition advice.

If you're actually trying to optimize your health, not just your weight, you need to think at a different level of resolution.

## The real question isn't "how many calories?"

It's "am I actually getting what my body needs?"

Calories can answer whether you're eating enough or too much. They can't answer whether you're *nourished*. And in a world where you can be overfed and undernourished at the same time - where calorie-dense, nutrient-poor food is the default option at every turn - the micronutrient question is the one worth asking.

I'm not saying stop counting calories if that's working for you. I'm saying it's the floor, not the ceiling. The interesting stuff - the stuff that actually determines how you feel, how you recover, how you age - is happening at the micronutrient level.

And if you're not tracking it, you're flying blind.
